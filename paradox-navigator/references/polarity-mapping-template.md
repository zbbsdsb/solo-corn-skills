# Polarity Mapping Template

This template provides a structured approach to mapping and managing any polarity you face as a founder. Follow the instructions to create a polarity map specific to your situation.

---

## Instructions for Filling Out the Template

### Step 1: Identify the Polarity

Name the two poles of the tension you are experiencing. Be specific:

**Good**: "Speed vs. Quality"
**Bad**: "Things are hard"

The polarity should be stated as a tension between two approaches, values, or priorities. Both poles should be things you actually want—situations where getting more of one is good up to a point.

### Step 2: Define Each Pole Precisely

For each pole, write a one-sentence definition that captures what it means to operate from this pole. Include the operational implication, not just the abstract concept.

**Pole A Definition Template**: "When we prioritize Pole A, we [specific action or characteristic]. This means [consequence for the organization]."

**Pole B Definition Template**: "When we prioritize Pole B, we [specific action or characteristic]. This means [consequence for the organization]."

### Step 3: Map the Positive Outcomes

For each pole, identify the specific positive outcomes that occur when you are operating successfully in that pole. Be concrete:

**For Pole A:**
- What benefits do you experience?
- What do customers or the team notice?
- What metrics improve?

**For Pole B:**
- What benefits do you experience?
- What do customers or the team notice?
- What metrics improve?

### Step 4: Map the Negative Consequences

For each pole, identify what goes wrong when you stay in that pole too long without checking in with the other. These are the compounding downsides.

**For Pole A Extreme:**
- What breaks when we over-index on Pole A?
- What do customers complain about?
- What does the team struggle with?
- What metrics deteriorate?

**For Pole B Extreme:**
- What breaks when we over-index on Pole B?
- What do customers complain about?
- What does the team struggle with?
- What metrics deteriorate?

### Step 5: Assess Current State

Describe honestly where you are now on the polarity spectrum. Consider:

- Which pole have you been operating from predominantly?
- How long have you been there?
- Are you starting to see the negative consequences of this pole?
- Have you already experienced the negative consequences?

Use a scale of 1-10:
- 1 = Fully in Pole B, Pole A completely neglected
- 10 = Fully in Pole A, Pole B completely neglected
- 5 = Balanced, oscillating appropriately

### Step 6: Determine Target State

Based on your diagnosis, describe where you need to move. The target is usually the pole you have been neglecting, moving away from the pole whose negative consequences are starting to show.

Be specific about the degree of shift:
- "Move moderately toward Pole B" (from 8 to 5)
- "Move significantly toward Pole A" (from 7 to 3)

### Step 7: Define Trigger Conditions

What specific signals will indicate it is time to shift back toward the other pole? These should be observable, concrete signals—not feelings.

**Trigger Template**: "When we observe [specific signal], it will be time to begin shifting back toward [other pole]."

Include:
- 2-3 leading indicators of negative consequences from current pole
- 1-2 metrics that will improve as you make the shift
- A time horizon if applicable (e.g., "after three months")

### Step 8: Plan Specific Shift Actions

List the specific, concrete actions you will take to shift toward the target pole. These should be:
- Actionable (someone can execute them)
- Time-bound (have a deadline)
- Observable (you can verify they happened)

**Shift Actions Template**:
1. [Specific action with owner and deadline]
2. [Specific action with owner and deadline]
3. [Specific action with owner and deadline]

### Explanation of This Example

**Why This Situation Developed:**
The team had been in "Speed" mode for four months, which was appropriate during a critical market entry phase. Competitors were emerging, and the first-mover advantage was real. However, the warning signals indicated that the negatives of this extreme were beginning to compound—customer satisfaction was declining, and engineers were becoming concerned.

**Why the Shift Was the Right Call:**
At position 8/10, the team was approaching the zone where speed's benefits begin to be outweighed by its costs. The bug-related churn could undermine the very market position the speed strategy was designed to capture. A moderate shift to 5/10 would preserve enough speed to remain competitive while addressing the accumulating quality concerns.

**Why Not All the Way to Quality:**
A shift to 2/10 or 1/10 (extreme quality focus) would risk:
- Missing the competitive window while we perfect the product
- Team morale impact from a dramatic change in direction
- Investor concern about slowing down when traction is building

**The Review Trigger:**
The trigger conditions are specific and measurable. "Competitor moves fast" is not just a feeling—it can be observed. "NPS returns to 42" is a specific metric with a defined target. This makes the shift reversible and evidence-based rather than based on intuition alone.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

### Mistake 1: Naming the Polarity Too Broadly

**Bad**: "Growth vs. Stability"
**Good**: "Growth velocity vs. Operational stability"

The more specific you are about what each pole means operationally, the more useful the map becomes.

### Mistake 2: Listing Vague Consequences

**Bad**: "We would lose focus"
**Good**: "Enterprise sales cycle would lengthen from 30 days to 60+ days, causing two deals in pipeline to slip to next quarter"

Specific, quantified consequences are more actionable and more recognizable when they appear.

### Mistake 3: Setting an Impossible Target

**Bad**: "Move to 5/10 immediately and stay there forever"
**Good**: "Move to 5/10 over the next 6 weeks, then reassess"

Balance is not a permanent state—it is a dynamic capability. Setting a target of "5/10 forever" misunderstands the goal.

### Mistake 4: Listing Actions Without Owners

Every shift action must have a single owner who is accountable for execution. If an action does not have an owner, it will not happen.

### Mistake 5: Skipping the Review Date

The review date is what transforms this document from a plan into a management tool. Without a specific review date, the map will sit on a shelf and not be used.

## Adapting the Template

This template is designed to be adapted to your specific needs:

**For Simpler Situations**: If the polarity is less severe, you can use a shorter version with just the key elements: Pole definitions, current state, target state, and one or two shift actions.

**For More Complex Situations**: If multiple stakeholders are involved, add a "communication plan" section describing how you will share the polarity map and shift plan with affected parties.

**For Recurring Polarities**: Once you have mapped a polarity successfully, keep the completed map as a reference. Update it when conditions change significantly, or when you complete a full oscillation and return to a similar state.

## Integrating With Your Workflow

### When to Create a Polarity Map

- When a difficult decision feels like "damned if I do, damned if I don't"
- When you have tried to resolve a tension three times and it keeps returning
- When your team is experiencing whiplash from direction changes
- When you notice yourself consistently avoiding one side of a tension

### Where to Store the Map

Store completed polarity maps in a shared location accessible to affected stakeholders. Consider:
- Your project management tool
- A shared drive with your leadership team
- A dedicated "polarity management" folder

### When to Update the Map

- At the scheduled review date
- When trigger conditions are met
- When the external environment changes significantly (new competitor, market shift)
- When results indicate the shift is not working as planned

The polarity map is a living document, not a one-time exercise. Its value comes from regular review and updating based on actual observations.
