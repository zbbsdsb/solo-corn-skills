# System Archetypes: Recurring Patterns in Startup Ecosystems

## Introduction

System archetypes are patterns that appear repeatedly across different systems. Named and catalogued by the systems thinking community, they provide a diagnostic vocabulary for identifying what's happening in your startup and a repertoire of intervention patterns that have worked in similar situations.

This document presents ten essential system archetypes, adapted for solo founders and small startup teams. For each archetype, you'll find diagnostic questions to identify it in your context, common founder manifestations, and intervention patterns that work.

---

## Archetype 1: Limits to Growth

### Description

A reinforcing loop drives growth, but a balancing loop eventually dominates, creating an S-curve. The growth slows not because resources run out but because something counteracts the growth.

### Structure

```
Reinforcing Loop (Growth)
    R
      ↗
        ↗ [Limiting Factor] (-)
          ↘
            Balancing Loop (Slows Growth)
```

### Diagnostic Questions

- Is your growth curve shaped like an S rather than exponential?
- Do you see early success that gradually slows despite continued effort?
- Are there symptoms that appear only after significant growth?
- Does adding more resources (people, money, time) produce diminishing returns?

### Founder Manifestations

- **Feature factory stall**: Adding features increases complexity, which reduces usability, which slows user growth despite more features.
- **Market saturation**: More users increase network value, attracting users until the non-users who might join are exhausted.
- **Team scaling crunch**: More hires produce more communication overhead, which reduces velocity per person.

### Intervention Pattern

1. **Identify the limiting factor**: What is actually preventing continued growth? (Often different from what you think.)
2. **Remove or weaken the constraint**: Don't add more resources to the growing part; address the limiting factor.
3. **Resist the temptation to amplify the reinforcing loop**: More growth hormones won't help if the bottleneck is elsewhere.
4. **Look for paradigms**: Sometimes the "limit" is a belief that can be shifted. ("We can't serve enterprise customers because...")

## Archetype 2: Shifting the Burden

### Description

A problem symptom is treated with a solution that produces side effects, creating dependence on the solution. The underlying problem remains or worsens, requiring ever stronger doses of the solution.

### Structure

```
Problem Symptom ←── B (Balancing Loop)
    ↑              ↑
    │              └── Intervention (Eases Symptom)
    │
    └── R (Reinforcing Loop, Problem Persists)
```

### Diagnostic Questions

- Do you find yourself solving the same problem repeatedly?
- Does the "solution" require increasing doses to achieve the same effect?
- Are side effects of the solution becoming more problematic?
- Is there a fundamental fix you've been deferring in favor of symptomatic treatment?

### Founder Manifestations

- **Technical debt accumulation**: Adding workarounds instead of refactoring. The workarounds require more workarounds.
- **Discounting to close deals**: Lowering price instead of improving value proposition. Each discount requires a further discount.
- **Hiring to solve capacity problems**: Adding people instead of improving process. More people create more coordination overhead.
- **Burnout from overwork**: Working more hours instead of improving efficiency. Exhaustion reduces effectiveness, requiring more hours.

### Intervention Pattern

1. **Recognize symptomatic solutions**: Ask "what problem does this really solve?"
2. **Separate symptomatic from fundamental solutions**: Explicitly allocate time to addressing root causes.
3. **Make fundamental solutions more attractive**: Remove barriers to doing things right.
4. **Strengthen information about side effects**: Make the long-term costs visible.
5. **Consider removing the symptomatic solution**: Sometimes the only way to force a fundamental fix is to stop the quick fix.

## Archetype 3: Tragedy of the Commons

### Description

Individual actors rationally optimize their own interests, but collectively deplete a shared resource. Each actor benefits from their action while the cost is shared by all.

### Structure

```
Individual Gain (+) → Action Taken (+) → Resource Depleted (-) → Individual Gain
         ↑                                                   ↓
         └───────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Diagnostic Questions

- Is there a shared resource that individuals are depleting for personal gain?
- Do you see behavior that benefits the individual but harms the collective?
- Are those who conserve the resource subsidizing those who exploit it?
- Does the resource show signs of degradation over time?

### Founder Manifestations

- **Engineer time as a commons**: Each feature team adds technical debt that slows all teams, but each team benefits from shipping features.
- **Brand reputation as a commons**: Sales team over-promises to close deals, damaging brand reputation that affects all future sales.
- **Team morale as a commons**: Founders take bad meetings that exhaust the team, reducing everyone's energy and creativity.
- **User trust as a commons**: Growth tactics that sacrifice trust damage the product for all users.

### Intervention Pattern

1. **Make the shared resource visible**: Quantify what's being depleted and by whom.
2. **Align individual incentives with collective good**: Create metrics that reward resource conservation.
3. **Establish clear boundaries**: Define who has access to what and under what conditions.
4. **Create feedback**: Make depletion visible in real time to those doing the depleting.
5. **Consider privatization**: Sometimes the only solution is to divide the commons into private holdings.

## Archetype 4: Drifting Goals

### Description

Performance standards gradually decline over time. The "acceptable" level of performance slowly erodes, often because the system was performing well in the past and standards are relaxed as problems accumulate.

### Structure

```
Actual Performance (-) → Standard Accepted as Acceptable (+) → Performance Expectations (-)
         ↑                                                     ↑
         │                                                     │
         └────────────────────── (Feedback Loop) ──────────────┘
```

### Diagnostic Questions

- Have your success metrics degraded gradually without explicit decisions to change them?
- Do you find yourself redefining "good enough" over time?
- Is there pressure to "be realistic" about what you can achieve?
- Do past successes serve as a reference that current performance is measured against?

### Founder Manifestations

- **Product quality drift**: Bugs that would have been unacceptable become "known issues." Tech debt that would have been addressed becomes "just how we do things."
- **Market positioning drift**: A premium product gradually becomes a discount option as pricing pressure accumulates.
- **Team standard drift**: The caliber of hires gradually declines as "good enough" hiring fills urgent needs.

### Intervention Pattern

1. **Make historical performance visible**: Compare current metrics to historical baselines, not just to each other.
2. **Fix the standard, not just the performance**: Define what "good enough" actually means and hold to it.
3. **Separate optimistic from realistic**: Don't let goal-setting become an exercise in lowering expectations.
4. **Create external benchmarks**: Compare your performance to competitors or industry standards.

## Archetype 5: Escalation

### Description

Two actors respond to each other in a way that escalates the threat or cost to both. Each escalation by one actor triggers a greater escalation by the other, spiraling toward a crisis.

### Structure

```
Actor A's Action (+) → Actor B's Response (+) → Actor A's Action
         ↑                                                ↑
         └──────────────────── (Escalation Loop) ────────┘
```

### Diagnostic Questions

- Are you in a competitive dynamic where your actions trigger counter-actions?
- Does each move you make seem to necessitate a stronger response?
- Are resources being diverted to "stay ahead" in ways that can't be sustained?
- Is there a competitor whose behavior mirrors your own?

### Founder Manifestations

- **Pricing wars**: You lower price, competitor lowers price, you lower further. Both margins erode.
- **Feature competition**: You add feature X, competitor adds X plus Y, you add X plus Y plus Z. Both products become bloated.
- **Marketing spending escalation**: You increase ad spend, competitor increases ad spend, both CPAs rise.
- **Hiring counter-offers**: You counter-offer to retain an employee, competitor counters your counter, salaries inflate.

### Intervention Pattern

1. **Recognize escalation dynamics early**: The spiral is easier to stop early than late.
2. **Refuse to participate in escalation**: This requires accepting short-term disadvantage.
3. **Find non-competitive alternatives**: Shift to a dimension of competition that isn't zero-sum.
4. **Communicate boundaries**: Sometimes a clear statement of where you'll stop prevents the other party from continuing.
5. **Consider exit**: Sometimes the only winning move is not to play.

## Archetype 6: Success to the Successful

### Description

Winners get resources that make them more likely to win again. Losers lose resources that would have helped them compete. The gap widens over time until one actor dominates.

### Structure

```
Winner's Advantage (+) → Resource Allocation (+) → More Wins
         ↑                                              ↑
         │                                              │
         └────────────────────── (Feedback Loop) ───────┘
```

### Diagnostic Questions

- Do your best customers get better service, attracting more similar customers?
- Do successful products get more investment, producing more success?
- Do top performers get the best opportunities, making them more successful?
- Is there a widening gap between "winners" and "losers" in your ecosystem?

### Founder Manifestations

- **Platform winner-take-all**: Early users get more value because more people use the platform, attracting more users.
- **Talent concentration**: Your best people want to work with other best people, making the best team even better while others stagnate.
- **Investor concentration**: Successful investors get better deals, producing better returns, getting better deals.

### Intervention Pattern

1. **Create leveling mechanisms**: Introduce rules that give smaller players access to resources.
2. **Limit winner advantages**: Cap the benefits that can be accumulated.
3. **Design for pluralism**: Create structures where multiple winners can coexist.
4. **Subsidize the disadvantaged**: Intentionally allocate resources to those who are losing.

## Archetype 7: Attractiveness Principle

### Description

The system's behavior is attracted to a particular state (the attractor). Disturbances are temporary; the system returns to the attractor state. The attractor can be healthy or unhealthy.

### Structure

```
Current State → System Attractor (+)
    ↑              ↓
    └── (Feedback) ←
```

### Diagnostic Questions

- Does your system tend to return to a particular state despite interventions?
- Are there patterns that feel "inevitable" or "just how things are"?
- Do external shocks temporarily shift behavior before the system returns to baseline?
- Is the attractor state one you want or one you don't?

### Founder Manifestations

- **Gravity toward chaos**: Despite efforts to create process, the startup always returns to reactive, chaotic mode.
- **Gravity toward perfectionism**: Despite intentions to ship, work always returns to endless polishing.
- **Gravity toward crisis mode**: The startup always drifts toward firefighting, even after efforts to create calm.

### Intervention Pattern

1. **Identify the attractor**: What is the system "trying" to return to?
2. **Understand why**: What structure creates this attraction?
3. **Change the attractor**: Redesign the system so a different state becomes the attractor.
4. **Remove the pull**: If the current attractor is unhealthy, remove the mechanisms that create the pull.

## Archetype 8: Eroding Goals

### Description

This is a special case of drifting goals where the goal itself erodes under pressure. It's distinct from drifting goals because the erosion is driven by external pressure rather than internal relaxation.

### Structure

```
External Pressure (+) → Goal Reduction (+) → Lower Standard (+)
         ↑                                           ↓
         └──────────────────── (Feedback Loop) ←───┘
```

### Diagnostic Questions

- Are your goals being reduced due to external pressure rather than new information?
- Do you find yourself "negotiating down" goals in response to objections?
- Is the erosion of goals driven by stakeholders who want to avoid the work required to meet them?
- Do lower goals lead to lower performance that justifies further goal reduction?

### Founder Manifestations

- **Investor-driven goal erosion**: Revenue targets are reduced after a difficult quarter, making the next quarter's targets lower still.
- **Customer-driven feature cuts**: Core features are removed from roadmap under customer pressure, reducing product value and causing more pressure to cut.
- **Team capacity-driven scope reduction**: Scope is cut not because it's unnecessary but because the team is exhausted.

### Intervention Pattern

1. **Protect minimum standards**: Define non-negotiable floor below which goals cannot go.
2. **Separate pressure from information**: Distinguish between "this is harder than we thought" and "we just don't want to do it."
3. **Require explicit justification for goal changes**: Any reduction should require a written explanation.
4. **Tie goals to purpose**: Connect goals to the underlying "why" so erosion feels consequential.

## Archetype 9: Growth and Underinvestment

### Description

A reinforcing loop of growth creates demand that eventually exceeds capacity. The system underinvests in capacity because investment decisions are made based on current utilization, not future demand.

### Structure

```
Growth (+) → Demand (+) → Capacity Utilization (+) → Investment Decision (-)
    ↑                                                            ↓
    │                                                            ↓
    └──────────────────── (Underinvestment Loop) ←──────────────┘
```

### Diagnostic Questions

- Are you consistently operating at high capacity with no slack?
- Do you invest in infrastructure only when you're already in crisis?
- Does growth create pressure that leaves no time to invest in supporting future growth?
- Are your best people focused on growth rather than building capability?

### Founder Manifestations

- **Engineering capacity crunch**: Growing user base strains infrastructure; team is too busy firefighting to rebuild properly.
- **Founder bandwidth saturation**: Founder is so busy running current operations no time to work on strategy.
- **Customer support quality erosion**: Support team is reactive, no time to improve processes or prevent future issues.

### Intervention Pattern

1. **Protect investment capacity**: Explicitly reserve resources (time, money, people) for investment even when utilization is high.
2. **Anticipate demand**: Make investment decisions based on projected need, not current utilization.
3. **Build slack**: Systems with no slack cannot absorb shocks or invest in improvement.
4. **Set capacity targets below 100%**: Aim for 70-80% utilization to leave room for unexpected demand and investment.

## Archetype 10: Seeking the Wrong Goal

### Description

The system optimizes for a proxy measure that doesn't capture what actually matters. Success by the proxy measure leads to failure in what actually matters, which isn't measured.

### Structure

```
Proxy Metric ↑ ← Optimized For (+)
Actual Outcome ↓ ← Proxy Metric (+)
```

### Diagnostic Questions

- Are you measuring something because it's easy to measure rather than because it matters?
- Does improving your key metric sometimes make actual outcomes worse?
- Are there important outcomes that aren't being measured at all?
- Do people find ways to look good on the metric without improving actual performance?

### Founder Manifestations

- **Daily active users optimization**: Engineering creates notifications that boost DAU but reduce user satisfaction.
- **Revenue optimization**: Founder pushes high-revenue deals that require heavy support, reducing profitability.
- **User count focus**: Marketing acquires users who never engage, inflating numbers without improving product-market fit.
- **Shipped features**: Engineering is measured by features shipped, not by whether those features create value.

### Intervention Pattern

1. **Audit your metrics**: For each metric, ask what behavior it incentivizes and whether that behavior is desirable.
2. **Look for unmeasured outcomes**: What important things aren't being tracked?
3. **Add negative indicators**: Track not just what you want but what you don't want.
4. **Qualitative feedback**: Don't let quantitative metrics crowd out direct user contact.
5. **Occam's razor for metrics**: The simplest metric that captures what matters is better than a complex dashboard.

## Summary: Archetype Quick Reference

| Archetype | Key Question | Founder Test |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|
| Limits to Growth | Why is growth slowing despite effort? | Is there a limiting factor that isn't resources? |
| Shifting the Burden | Am I solving the same problem repeatedly? | Does the solution require ever larger doses? |
| Tragedy of the Commons | Who is depleting shared resources? | Does individual gain come at collective cost? |
| Drifting Goals | Has "good enough" slowly declined? | Compare today to a year ago, not last month. |
| Escalation | Is competition spiraling? | Am I responding to actions that warrant escalation? |
| Success to the Successful | Are winners getting all the resources? | Is the gap between best and worst widening? |
| Attractiveness Principle | What state does the system keep returning to? | Does change feel futile? |
| Eroding Goals | Are goals being reduced under pressure? | Am I confusing difficulty with wrongness? |
| Growth and Underinvestment | Am I too busy growing to invest in growth? | When did I last work on the business, not in it? |
| Seeking the Wrong Goal | Am I optimizing for the right thing? | Do my metrics capture what actually matters? |
