# Excavation Protocol: Surface the Hidden Assumptions

## Overview

Every startup plan is built on a foundation of assumptions. Some are explicit and tested; others are invisible until they collapse. The excavation protocol is a systematic methodology for surfacing, cataloging, and prioritizing these hidden assumptions before they sink your venture.

The founder's job is not to have all the answers—it is to know which questions matter most and to design experiments that answer them cheaply. This protocol gives you that systematic discipline.

---

## The Excavation Mindset

Before diving into the methodology, internalize these principles:

1. **Assumptions are not weaknesses.** Every plan contains assumptions. Acknowledging them is a sign of intellectual rigor, not failure.
2. **Undiscovered assumptions are the real risk.** You can mitigate known risks, but unknown unknowns destroy companies.
3. **Cheap validation beats expensive failure.** A $500 test today can prevent a $500,000 mistake tomorrow.
4. **Excavation is iterative.** You will not find everything on the first pass. Return to this protocol as your understanding deepens.
5. **The goal is prioritization, not completeness.** You cannot test everything. Surface the assumptions that, if wrong, would be most damaging.

## The 8 Layers of Assumptions

Assumptions organize into eight distinct layers, each requiring different interrogation strategies. Start from the center (Self) and work outward. The inner layers are often the most insidious because they are the hardest to see.

### Layer 1: Self Assumptions

These are the assumptions you make about yourself—your capabilities, your biases, and your blind spots. Founders frequently overestimate their ability to execute, underestimating the complexity of what they are building.

**Key Self Assumption Categories:**

- **Capability assumptions:** Can I actually build this? Do I have the technical skill, domain expertise, or operational experience required?
- **Commitment assumptions:** Will I stay engaged when the project becomes grueling? Are my motivations aligned with the demands?
- **Bias assumptions:** Am I seeing this clearly? Am I anchored on a past success or a pet theory? Is my enthusiasm clouding my judgment?
- **Network assumptions:** Do I have access to the people, capital, and resources I will need? Will advisors, investors, or partners take my calls?

**Self Assumption Questions:**

1. What skills does this venture require that I do not currently have?
2. What is the worst-case scenario for my personal engagement over a 3-year horizon?
3. If this fails, will I regret starting, or will I regret having tried?
4. What am I most likely wrong about in my current plan?
5. Who has succeeded at this before, and do I share their prerequisites?
6. What would have to be true for me to quit?
7. Am I solving this problem because I have unique insight, or because I find it personally interesting?
8. What biases have I not accounted for in my analysis?

---

### Layer 2: Team Assumptions

Your founding team and early hires carry assumptions about cohesion, capability distribution, and alignment. Many promising startups fail not because the idea was bad but because the team fractured.

**Key Team Assumption Categories:**

- **Capability distribution assumptions:** Does the team cover all critical domains? Who is responsible for what?
- **Alignment assumptions:** Do all founders want the same thing? Are we aligned on equity, exit timeline, and risk tolerance?
- **Decision-making assumptions:** How will we resolve disagreements? Is there a clear hierarchy for final decisions?
- **Commitment assumptions:** Will each team member dedicate the required time and energy? Are there competing priorities?
- **Cultural assumptions:** Can we work together under pressure? Have we collaborated in high-stakes situations before?

**Team Assumption Questions:**

1. What happens if one co-founder wants to quit after 6 months?
2. Have we explicitly discussed equity splits and vesting? Is this documented?
3. Who makes the final call on technical decisions? On product decisions? On hiring?
4. Do we have complementary skills, or are we all strong in the same area?
5. Have we worked together under real pressure before, or only in low-stakes environments?
6. What are the non-negotiables for each team member that we have not yet discussed?
7. Is there a clear escalation path when two team members disagree fundamentally?
8. What assumptions are we making about each team member's risk tolerance and commitment level?

---

### Layer 3: Market Assumptions

Market assumptions are often the most dangerous because they are based on macro trends, timing, and forces outside your control. The "total addressable market" slide kills more startups than competitive pressure ever does.

**Key Market Assumption Categories:**

- **Market existence assumptions:** Does this market actually exist? Are people currently spending money on解决这个问题?
- **Market size assumptions:** How large is the addressable market? What percentage can we realistically capture?
- **Market timing assumptions:** Is now the right time to enter? What secular trends make this viable today that were not true 5 years ago?
- **Market structure assumptions:** Is this market consolidating or fragmenting? Is there room for a new entrant?
- **Trend assumptions:** Are the trends we are betting on durable or temporary? Could regulatory changes, economic shifts, or technological disruptions reverse them?

**Market Assumption Questions:**

1. What is the minimum viable version of this market that would make our business work?
2. What would have to be true for the market to be 10x larger than our current estimate?
3. Who is the marginal customer today, and what would cause them to switch from existing solutions?
4. What market dynamics could shift against us in the next 18 months?
5. Are we mistaking a trend for a durable structural shift?
6. What regulatory, economic, or technological changes could eliminate this market entirely?
7. What percentage of the current market is captive to incumbents who would be difficult to displace?
8. Is this market growing, stable, or declining? What data supports this?

---

### Layer 4: Customer Assumptions

Understanding customers is the difference between building in a vacuum and building something people actually want. Customer assumptions cover who your users are, what they need, and what they will actually pay for.

**Key Customer Assumption Categories:**

- **User identification assumptions:** Do we know who our target user is? Have we actually spoken to them?
- **Problem existence assumptions:** Is this problem real and significant enough for users to seek a solution?
- **Problem prioritization assumptions:** Is solving this problem among the top 3 priorities for our target users?
- **Willingness to pay assumptions:** Will customers pay our target price? Have they paid similar prices for comparable solutions?
- **Accessibility assumptions:** Can we reach our target customers efficiently? Do they congregate somewhere we can find them?
- **Retention assumptions:** Will customers stick with our solution long-term, or will they churn when the novelty wears off?

**Customer Assumption Questions:**

1. Have we talked to at least 20 real target users in the past 90 days?
2. What is the exact problem we are solving, and how do users currently solve it today?
3. What would a perfect day look like for our target user, and how does our solution affect that?
4. What is the minimum feature set that would make users switch from their current solution?
5. How much time or money do users currently spend on this problem?
6. What would cause a user to abandon our product after the first month?
7. Are we solving a symptom or a root cause? Would removing this problem significantly improve our users' lives?
8. Do our target users have the budget authority to purchase our solution, or do they need to convince someone else?

---

### Layer 5: Competitor Assumptions

Never assume you have no competitors. If you think you have no competition, you have not looked hard enough. Competitor assumptions cover how you differentiate, how incumbents will respond, and what barriers you will build.

**Key Competitor Assumption Categories:**

- **Competitor identification assumptions:** Who are our direct competitors? Who are our indirect competitors? Who could become a competitor within 18 months?
- **Competitive advantage assumptions:** What is our sustainable differentiation? Why can we win where others have failed?
- **Incumbent response assumptions:** How will existing market leaders respond when we gain traction? Can they simply copy our approach?
- **Switching cost assumptions:** What does it cost customers to switch from their current solution to ours? Is this barrier too high to overcome?
- **Moat durability assumptions:** Can we build defenses that get stronger over time? What is the half-life of our competitive advantage?

**Competitor Assumption Questions:**

1. Who has tried to solve this problem before, and why did they succeed or fail?
2. What would our top 3 competitors do if we launched tomorrow?
3. What is our unfair advantage, and how long until competitors can replicate it?
4. What switching costs must we overcome, and how will we overcome them?
5. Could a well-funded competitor crush us by simply outspending us on product and marketing?
6. Are we positioning against the right competitor, or are we fighting the wrong battle?
7. What is the minimum differentiated capability that would make customers try us?
8. Will our competitors view us as a threat worth responding to, or are we too small to notice?

---

### Layer 6: Technology Assumptions

Technology assumptions cover whether your product can be built, whether it will scale, and whether the technical choices you make today will remain valid as you grow.

**Key Technology Assumption Categories:**

- **Build feasibility assumptions:** Can we actually build this with available technology? Is there an unsolved technical problem at our core?
- **Scalability assumptions:** Will the architecture support our projected growth? At what point will we hit scaling walls?
- **Technical dependency assumptions:** Are we reliant on third-party infrastructure, APIs, or platforms that could change?
- **Performance assumptions:** Will the system be fast enough? Reliable enough? Secure enough?
- **Technical debt assumptions:** Are we making shortcuts that will compound into future problems?

**Technology Assumption Questions:**

1. What is the single hardest technical problem in our product, and have we proven we can solve it?
2. At what user or data volume will our architecture break down?
3. What third-party services or infrastructure are we dependent on, and what happens if they change pricing or availability?
4. What is the expected latency, uptime, and error rate for our core user interactions?
5. How will we handle security and data privacy as we scale?
6. Are we building on a technology stack that will still be viable in 5 years?
7. What technical decisions are we deferring that will become expensive to change later?
8. Have we validated our technical approach with someone who has built at our target scale?

---

### Layer 7: Resource Assumptions

Resource assumptions cover the capital, talent, time, and partnerships you need to execute your plan. Most startups fail not from bad strategy but from running out of runway.

**Key Resource Assumption Categories:**

- **Capital assumptions:** How much money do we need, and when will we need it? Can we raise it on our current terms?
- **Talent assumptions:** Can we attract the people we need at our current stage and compensation?
- **Time assumptions:** How long will execution actually take? Are our timelines realistic?
- **Partnership assumptions:** Do we need strategic partners to succeed, and can we actually secure those partnerships?
- **Operational assumptions:** Do we have the infrastructure, tools, and processes to operate efficiently?

**Resource Assumption Questions:**

1. What is our burn rate, and how many months of runway do we have? What triggers would cause us to burn faster than projected?
2. What is our plan if we cannot raise the next round on our current timeline?
3. What are the critical hires we cannot succeed without, and what is our plan if we cannot fill those roles?
4. Are our timelines based on best-case scenarios or realistic estimates with buffers?
5. What partnerships or vendor relationships are essential to our success, and how likely are we to secure them?
6. What operational bottlenecks will we hit at 10x, 100x our current scale?
7. What resources do we need in the next 6 months that we do not currently have access to?
8. If our revenue projections are off by 50%, can we still survive for 12 months?

---

### Layer 8: Timeline Assumptions

Timeline assumptions are the stealth killer of startups. Founders consistently underestimate how long things take and overestimate what can be accomplished in a given period.

**Key Timeline Assumption Categories:**

- **Development timeline assumptions:** How long will it actually take to build the product?
- **Revenue timeline assumptions:** When will we achieve revenue? Profitability?
- **Growth timeline assumptions:** How long until we achieve meaningful market traction?
- **Milestone assumptions:** What are the critical inflection points, and are our timeline estimates realistic?
- **Contingency assumptions:** What happens to our plan if a key milestone is delayed by 3, 6, or 12 months?

**Timeline Assumption Questions:**

1. What is the single longest lead-time item in our plan, and have we accounted for it properly?
2. How many similar companies have taken, on average, to reach our target milestone?
3. What percentage of our milestones have we hit on time in the past? Does this inform our current estimates?
4. What is the minimum viable version of our product, and what is the fastest realistic path to building it?
5. If we double all our time estimates, does our plan still make sense?
6. What external dependencies (partnerships, regulatory approvals, third-party development) could delay us?
7. At what point does a delay in our timeline trigger a change in strategy?
8. What is our plan if we are 6 months behind schedule at the 12-month mark?

## The Excavation Session Format

Run excavation sessions in 90-minute blocks with your full team. Use this format:

### Before the Session (10 minutes preparation)

1. Distribute the assumption layer descriptions to all participants 24 hours in advance.
2. Assign each participant to come with 3 assumptions per layer they are responsible for.
3. Prepare a shared document with all 8 layers clearly delineated.

### During the Session (90 minutes)

| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 0-10 min | Set the context. Remind the team of the plan's core thesis and the stakes of undiscovered assumptions. |
| 10-30 min | Surface assumptions layer by layer. Use sticky notes or a shared document. One assumption per note. No judgment, no consolidation yet. |
| 30-50 min | Consolidate and deduplicate. Group similar assumptions. Identify the most surprising or uncomfortable assumptions. |
| 50-70 min | Prioritize using the 2x2 matrix (see Assumption Taxonomy). Each person gets 3 votes. Place top assumptions on the high-impact, low-confidence quadrant. |
| 70-90 min | Assign ownership and design first-pass validation experiments. Document the top 5-10 assumptions to test first. |

### After the Session

1. Distribute the consolidated assumption list to all stakeholders.
2. Schedule bi-weekly excavation review sessions to revisit and update assumptions.
3. Track all assumptions in the dashboard (see Dashboard Template).

## Principles for Founders

1. **You will not find everything the first time.** Return to excavation regularly as your understanding evolves.
2. **The uncomfortable assumptions are usually the most important.** Pay special attention to assumptions that make you defensive or dismissive.
3. **Not all assumptions are equal.** Focus on the ones where being wrong would be most damaging.
4. **Testing beats analysis.** When in doubt, design a cheap experiment before launching a deep analysis.
5. **Document everything.** Undocumented assumptions are impossible to track, review, or learn from.
6. **Update your beliefs.** When you discover you were wrong about an assumption, update your mental model and adjust your plan.
